Technical projects

Discovery Project feature · ECE 1100, Fall 2026 · In progress

Teaching a microcontroller to hear a failing fan

My Discovery Project will put a small neural network on an STM32 microcontroller that listens to a fan's vibration and flags faults in real time, with no computer or cloud connection involved. This page describes the plan. I'll replace it with the build, photos, and results before the Discovery Project Showcase.

The problem

Rotating machines rarely fail all at once. A fan blade picks up debris, a mount works loose, or a bearing starts to wear, and the vibration signature changes long before anything breaks. Factories catch this with predictive maintenance: sensors stream data to a computer that looks for warning signs. That works on a factory floor with Wi-Fi and power to spare. It doesn't work on a robot or a spacecraft, where every reaction wheel and actuator is a rotating machine and there's no server nearby to ask.

My question is whether a cheap microcontroller, the kind of chip already sitting inside most robots, can make that call by itself. A machine that can diagnose its own hardware is one step closer to the autonomy space robots need.

The plan

I'll mount an accelerometer (a sensor that measures vibration as changes in acceleration) to a fan and record it running normally and with deliberate faults, such as an unbalanced blade or a loose mount. Each recording gets cut into short windows and converted with a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), which breaks a signal into the frequencies it's made of. Different faults should show up as energy at different frequencies, which gives a neural network something meaningful to learn from.

I'll train a small network in Python to classify each window, building on the work I've been doing rebuilding neural networks from scratch with Andrej Karpathy's micrograd series. The hardest constraint is size: a microcontroller has kilobytes of memory, not gigabytes. To fit, I plan to keep the network small and convert its weights to 8-bit integers (quantization), which shrinks the model and speeds it up at a small cost in accuracy, before deploying it to the STM32.

Why this project

I picked it on purpose to stretch skills I don't use elsewhere. My YJSP work is state estimation in simulation, so this project pushes me toward embedded C on real hardware and toward deploying machine learning, not just training it. Both are exactly what space robotics demands.

How I'll measure success

Accuracy on recordings the network never saw during training, inference time on the chip, and whether the model fits in the STM32's memory with room to spare. Results, photos of the build, and a demo video will go here before the Showcase.

Other work

  • Yellow Jacket Space Program · Avionics software, guidance, navigation and control · September 2026 to present

    Rocket state estimation

    A rocket's flight computer has to know its altitude, velocity, and orientation to deploy recovery at the right moment, and every sensor it carries is noisy. Our team fuses those sensors with a 22-state Extended Kalman Filter (EKF), an algorithm that combines a physics model of the rocket with sensor readings and weighs each one by how much it can be trusted. I own the magnetometer measurement model in our MATLAB software-in-the-loop simulation, which tests flight software against simulated flights before anything flies. That means translating the sensor's datasheet into realistic behavior, including noise, quantization (readings snapped to discrete steps), saturation (readings capped at the sensor's limits), and thermal drift, validated across a 105,000-sample simulated flight.

    Two findings stand out. By comparing our firmware configuration against the datasheet, I found we were underestimating magnetometer noise by 33%, which meant the filter assumed its measurements were about 4× more trustworthy than they really were. I also diagnosed a framework defect that was silently running every sensor model with zero noise, which made our filter tests look far better than reality. Separately, I modeled Earth's magnetic field along the rocket's 3D trajectory using the World Magnetic Model (the standard model of Earth's magnetic field, published by NOAA) at about 95× lower compute cost than our previous approach. Next, I'm developing a backup filter so the vehicle can still recover if the primary estimate fails.

  • Georgia Tech Extended Reality · Robot Dog team · August 2026 to present

    Quadruped locomotion on a Unitree Go2

    I'm implementing locomotion control and position estimation on a Unitree Go2 quadruped, and deploying pre-trained reinforcement learning policies for gait and balance onto the physical robot. Reinforcement learning (RL) trains a controller through trial and error in simulation; the hard part is getting that behavior to hold up on a real robot with real motors and real floors. The work runs on ROS2 (Robot Operating System 2, the standard middleware that lets a robot's software components talk to each other), which bridges our trained models to an onboard NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano computer.

  • Vertically Integrated Projects · Agentic AI research · Fall 2026 to present

    Better AI for presenting academic papers

    Agentic AI systems are models that plan and take multi-step actions toward a goal instead of answering a single question. Our research asks how to make AI better at turning dense academic papers into clear presentations, a task that requires understanding the paper, deciding what matters, and communicating it to a specific audience. Our team is in the process of publishing a paper on this work.

  • Independent project · July 2026 to present

    Autodifferentiation engine and neural network library

    Autodifferentiation is how a neural network computes the gradients it needs to learn. I built a scalar autograd engine from scratch in Python, using operator overloading and a dynamic computational graph, with backpropagation done by topologically sorting the graph and accumulating gradients through the chain rule. I verified my gradients against PyTorch's, extended the engine with new operations, and then trained a multilayer perceptron end to end on it, writing the loss function and gradient descent updates myself.

Career goals

Long term, I want to build the software and electronics that let robots work on their own in space: robots that service satellites, assemble structures in orbit, and explore surfaces where a human operator is minutes away by radio.

The companies doing this most aggressively right now are SpaceX and Blue Origin on the launch and spacecraft side, and Tesla on the humanoid robotics side with Optimus. Those two worlds are converging. Space robotics needs the same things humanoid robots need: reliable embedded software, accurate state estimation, and learned controllers that can be trusted on real hardware. My plan is to build depth in exactly that overlap.

I'm specializing in the Robotics & Autonomous Systems and Computing Hardware & Emerging Architectures (CHEA) threads. Threads are Georgia Tech's way of building a degree around two focus areas, and these two cover both halves of what I want to do: the robots themselves, and the hardware they run on.
